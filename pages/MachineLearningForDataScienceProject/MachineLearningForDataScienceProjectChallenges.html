CSCI 5612 Machine Learning for Data Science:
Regional Climate Factor Analysis:
Challenges

This page will cover some of the same information as the Results page, which included an analysis of the outputs and performances of the models used in this project. For this section however, there will be a deeper look at the supervised learning models. The work done before this point, encompassing Clustering and PCA, was relatively lackluster. Its coverage on the Results page is as much attention as should be given to it, based on the usefulness of its results.

The first challenge to discuss is the data cleaning process. Compared to some other datasets, even those used in class for assignments, the EPI dataset was not difficult to prepare. An early decision was made to reduce the dataset from a time series, going back to 1990 with increasingly sparse data collection, into a single snapshot of the years 2019-2020. This provided the widest coverage of features and allowed the project to focus on one year, rather than spreading the attention to time series analysis. Following the aggregation of each of the features into one dataframe, the cleaning process was comprised of:
• Replacing obviously incorrect values, such as -9999.
• Filling NA values with the column mean (as removing any rows would mean removing an entire country's representation).
• Dropping completely empty columns, which was not a substantial difference.

An addition was made to the dataframe by the time that supervised learning was started: the ND-GAIN Index. This score measured a country's vulnerability to and readiness for climate change, and proved to be a more relevant, applicable, and interesting target variable compared to climate model predictions. This score was trivial to obtain and add to the dataframe though, as it is made easily accessible by Notre Dame.

The supervised learning models presented fairly few challenges. The dataset was already cleaned, and features were easily handled by each of the models. Simple transformers, such as for encoding labels, were used without difficulty. The greatest challenge came in assessing the models. As discussed on the results page, each model presented a different sort of specialization in predicting a country's risk according to the ND-GAIN Index. Solely going by the performance metrics of each model, the Decision Tree performed worst (F1-score 0.76), the Naive Bayes model performed moderately well (F1-score 0.81), and the Gradient Boosting model performed best (F1-score 0.84). However, this did not capture the detail that the decision tree had perfect performance in predicting whether a country would be at high risk from climate change. This is an important prediction to be able to make, which meant that the decision tree was given more importance than its meager performance metrics would have indicated. Each of the models were extensively tuned to optimize their performance, and of the tests conducted with the Decision Tree and Gradient Boosting models, their highest metric-based performances coincided with predicting high risk extremely well. Seeing a specialization in another category with these models would have represented a substantial loss in predictive power compared to the finals forms shown for this project.

Overall, this project was not held back by substantial technical challenges. The initial dataset and ND-GAIN feature were not the intended focus of the project, which led to confusion in the initial stages of the project. However, these datasets proved to be much more novel and interesting to analyze than the original idea, based on simple climate statistics, likely would have been. Otherwise, the surprise described above and elsewhere with the unique results of the supervised learning models presented something of a challenge for analysis and the determination of real model performance as opposed to metric-based analysis.
